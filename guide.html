The Doctor Isn't Listening to You

You tried to explain what is happening. They cut you off, looked at the computer, dismissed it, or moved on. You leave feeling like you wasted your one chance. This is one of the most common, exhausting experiences in American healthcare — and there is a way to be heard without being rude, without losing your cool, and without giving up.

Truth
You know your body better than they do. Being a confident reporter is not being a difficult patient.
Before the visit
Write down your top three concerns. Bring the paper. Hand it to them if you need to.
If they still don't listen
You have the right to a second opinion or a different provider. Always.
What YOU do
1
Write your top 3 concerns on paper before the appointment
Most doctor visits are 7-15 minutes. If you walk in without notes, you will forget what mattered most. Three is the right number — more and you lose focus, fewer and you may not cover what you came for.
2
Lead with the most important concern
Do not save the big thing for the end. Doctors often run out of time. Start the visit with: "Today I most need to talk about [thing]." This sets the agenda before they do.
3
If they cut you off, calmly ask to finish
"Hold on — I want to finish describing what I am experiencing, because the details matter." This is not rude. This is what good patients do. Most doctors, when reminded, will let you continue.
4
Ask for your concern to be documented in the chart
If they dismiss something you think matters, ask: "Can you put in the chart that I reported [symptom] and that we decided not to investigate further?" This often changes the doctor's behavior — and protects you if the symptom turns out to matter later.
5
Bring someone with you if you can
A second person in the room changes everything. They can take notes, ask follow-up questions, and serve as a witness. This is especially important if you have been dismissed before or if you have any kind of communication barrier.
6
If you leave still not heard, you can do something
Message the office through the patient portal that same day. Ask for a follow-up visit. Ask for a referral to a specialist. Ask for your records. If the pattern continues, you can change providers — and you can file a complaint with your insurance company, the practice, or the state medical board if dismissal led to harm.
What to SAY
1
Opening the visit
"Before we get started, I want you to know — today I most need to talk about [main concern]. I have two other things I want to mention too, but the first one is the priority."
2
If they interrupt you mid-sentence
"Let me finish describing this — I think the details are going to matter for figuring out what's going on."
3
If they dismiss your concern
"I hear that you think this isn't serious. I want to make sure it's documented in my chart that I reported [symptom] on this date. And I'd like to understand — what would make you reconsider? What should I watch for?"
4
If you want a second opinion
"I would like to get a second opinion on this before we decide on a treatment plan. Can you help me arrange a referral, or should I find one on my own?"
5
When you've been talked over and feel small
Take a slow breath. Then: "I want to pause for a second. I came here today because I am concerned about something, and I need to feel like that concern is being taken seriously. Can we slow down?"
If the pattern continues
You can switch primary care doctors at any time. Your insurance company has a list of in-network providers — call the number on the back of your card
Federally Qualified Health Centers (findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov) serve everyone regardless of ability to pay, and often offer more time per visit
If dismissal led to a missed diagnosis or harm, you can file a complaint with your state medical board (search "[your state] medical board complaint")
For complex or rare conditions, patient advocacy organizations specific to that condition often connect people with doctors who specialize in listening
If language is part of the barrier, federal law requires medical interpreters at no cost to you — ask for one
You are the expert on your own body. They are the expert on medicine. Both are needed.
Good medical care is a partnership. When a doctor does not listen, the partnership breaks — and the patient is the one who loses, every time. Speaking up clearly, calmly, and persistently is not difficult-patient behavior. It is the behavior of someone who knows their body and takes their own health seriously. The best doctors welcome a patient who comes prepared. The doctors who do not — those are the ones you may need to leave behind. Both are okay.

You Can't Afford the Medication They Prescribed

You left the doctor's office with a prescription. At the pharmacy counter, the price is more than you can pay. This is not a personal failure. Drug prices in the United States are designed to be confusing, and there are several real paths to bring the cost down — most people are never told about them. Here is what to do, right now, before you walk away empty-handed.

First thing
Do not just walk away. Tell the pharmacist you can't afford it.
Truth
The price you see is rarely the only price available. There are usually options.
Remember
Asking for a cheaper option is normal. Pharmacists handle this every day.
What YOU do
1
Tell the pharmacist before you leave the counter
Right at the counter, before you walk away, tell them the price is too high. They cannot help you if they do not know. This is the single most important step.
⚠ Walking away without saying anything is the most common mistake. The price will not change on its own.
2
Ask if there is a generic version
Generic medications are the same active ingredient as the brand name, usually at a fraction of the price. The pharmacist can tell you immediately if one exists for your prescription.
3
Ask the pharmacist to check GoodRx or a discount program
GoodRx, SingleCare, and similar discount programs often beat insurance prices. The pharmacist can run your medication through them right at the counter. This takes 30 seconds.
4
Ask if the manufacturer has a patient assistance program
Most major drug manufacturers run programs that give the medication free or at very low cost to people who can't afford it. The pharmacist may know, or you can search "[medication name] patient assistance program" on your phone.
5
If still too expensive, call your doctor's office before leaving
Tell them you can't afford the medication. Doctors can prescribe a cheaper alternative in the same drug class — but only if they know cost is the problem. Many will switch the prescription same-day.
6
If you have insurance, ask about a formulary exception
If your insurance is refusing to cover the medication, you can file a formulary exception or prior authorization appeal. The doctor's office handles this paperwork. Tell them you need them to file it.
What to SAY
1
At the pharmacy counter
"I can't afford this at this price. Is there a generic version, or can you check if GoodRx or another discount program has a lower price?"
2
If they say there's no generic
"Is there a manufacturer patient assistance program for this medication? Or do you know of another way to bring the price down?"
3
Calling the doctor's office
"I just left the pharmacy. I can't afford the medication Dr. [Name] prescribed — it's $[amount]. Can the doctor prescribe a less expensive alternative, or help me with patient assistance paperwork?"
4
If insurance denied coverage
"My insurance denied this prescription. Can you file a formulary exception or prior authorization on my behalf? I need this medication and I can't pay out of pocket."
5
If you feel embarrassed
Say it anyway. "I want to be honest — I can't afford this. What are my options?" Pharmacists hear this every single day. They are not judging you.
If the medication is for a serious condition and you still can't get it
Call 211 (free helpline) and ask about local medication assistance programs in your county
Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) often have sliding-scale pharmacy access — find one at findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov
For diabetes, asthma, and other chronic conditions, manufacturer programs are especially robust — search the drug name plus "patient assistance"
If this is a heart medication, insulin, or anything you cannot safely go without, tell the doctor's office it is an emergency cost issue — they have escalation paths
The system makes this hard on purpose. You are doing it right.
Prescription drug pricing in the United States is one of the most confusing systems by design. The price tag at the counter is rarely the real price — there are layers of discounts, coupons, assistance programs, and insurance options that pharmacies don't always volunteer. The fact that you had to ask does not mean you failed. It means the system did not tell you what was possible. Asking is exactly the right move. Most people who do this walk out with a price they can actually pay.

A Debt Collector Is Calling You

The phone rings. The voice on the other end is firm, sometimes aggressive, sometimes threatening. They say you owe money. You feel cornered. Before you say anything, before you agree to anything, before you give them a single piece of information — slow down. Federal law gives you specific rights in this conversation, and most collectors count on you not knowing what they are.

Right now
Do not confirm the debt. Do not agree to pay anything. Do not give them new information about yourself.
Your right
You can demand the debt be validated in writing before paying a cent. The law is on your side.
Never say
"Yes, that's my debt." Confirming it can restart the legal time clock against you.
What YOU do
1
Slow the call down — do not agree to anything
Whatever they are saying, your first move is to slow down. You are not required to confirm or deny anything. You are allowed to listen, take notes, and respond later.
🛑 Never give them your Social Security number, bank account, or credit card information on a cold call. Legitimate collectors will accept written communication.
2
Write down what they are telling you
Name of the collector. Name of the original creditor. Amount they claim you owe. Their phone number and address. Date of the call. You will need this. Take notes during the call or right after.
3
Demand a debt validation letter in writing
Under federal law (the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act), you have the right to request the debt be validated in writing within 30 days of first contact. Tell them you want a validation letter mailed to you. Until they send it, they cannot legally pressure you to pay.
4
Check whether the debt is past the statute of limitations
Old debts (typically 3-6 years depending on your state and debt type) cannot legally be sued for. Collectors often call about these anyway. Look up your state's statute of limitations for the type of debt before agreeing to anything. Even acknowledging an old debt can restart the clock.
5
If the debt is legitimate and recent, get free help before making a plan
Nonprofit credit counselors (find one at nfcc.org) can help you understand the debt and negotiate. Legal aid can help if you are being sued. Do not pay a "debt settlement company" that charges upfront fees — they are often scams.
6
If they harass you, you can stop them
Federal law limits when and how collectors can contact you. No calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. They cannot threaten arrest, lie about the debt, or contact your employer about it. You can send a cease-and-desist letter to stop all contact. File complaints with the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov.
What to SAY
1
When they first call
"Before we go any further — I need to verify this debt. Please send me a debt validation letter in writing with the original creditor, the amount, and documentation. I will not discuss this further until I receive that letter."
2
If they pressure you to pay now
"I am exercising my right under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act to receive validation of this debt in writing first. I will respond after I receive that letter."
3
If they threaten you
"Threatening me is a violation of federal law. I am ending this call. I will be filing a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau."
4
If they call you at work
"You are not permitted to contact me at this location. Do not call this number again." Federal law requires them to stop. Document the call and file a complaint.
5
If the debt is real and you want to negotiate
"I want to discuss a possible settlement. I cannot pay the full amount. What is the lowest amount you can accept in writing, and how will the debt be reported after it is resolved?" Get every agreement in writing before paying anything.
Your legal protections — use them
Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (consumerfinance.gov) accepts complaints about debt collectors and often gets results
Federal Trade Commission (reportfraud.ftc.gov) handles debt collection scams and harassment
Legal Services Corporation (lsc.gov/find-legal-aid) provides free legal help if you are being sued for a debt
National Foundation for Credit Counseling (nfcc.org) connects you with free nonprofit credit counseling
If a collector is using your incarceration history against you (common for re-entering citizens), that may violate fair debt practices — document and report
The collector knows the rules. Now you do too.
Debt collectors are professionals. They make their living convincing people to pay quickly, without thinking, without checking. The script they use is designed to make you feel cornered and ashamed. But federal law gives you specific rights — to verification, to documentation, to time to think, to stop the calls. The people who handle debt collection calls best are not the ones who pay fastest. They are the ones who slow it down, ask for everything in writing, and never agree to anything on a phone call. That is not avoidance. That is exactly how this is supposed to work.

Applying for SNAP, Medicaid, or Other Assistance

You need help. You are sitting down to apply for SNAP, Medicaid, TANF, WIC, housing assistance, or another program — and the form is long, the language is confusing, and the questions feel like traps. The system is built this way. Knowing how to move through it carefully protects you. Here is how to prepare, what to expect, and what to do when something does not match.

Truth
You are entitled to apply. Applying is not asking for charity — it is using a system you have already paid into.
Never do
Guess on questions you don't know. Write "unsure" or leave blank rather than guess wrong.
Always do
Keep a copy of every document you submit and write down every name and date you talk to anyone.
What YOU do
1
Gather documents before you start the form
You will be asked for: photo ID, Social Security card or number, proof of address (utility bill, lease), proof of income (pay stubs, benefits letters), proof of expenses (rent, utilities), and birth certificates for any children. Gather what you have. Missing documents do not stop the application — you can submit what you have and add more later.
2
Answer only what you know
If a question confuses you or asks for information you don't have, write "unknown" or "not sure" — do NOT guess. A wrong guess can be used against you later. An honest "unknown" cannot.
⚠ Forms often ask about income, assets, or household members in ways that seem clear but are not. When in doubt, ask the caseworker before answering.
3
Submit the application even if it feels incomplete
Submitting starts the timer on processing. You can add documents and clarify answers later — but only if the application is filed. An incomplete application that is submitted is better than a perfect application that sits on your kitchen table.
4
Write down your case number and the date you submitted
The case number is how every future conversation will start. Keep it somewhere safe — phone notes, a folder, written down somewhere you will not lose it.
5
If you are asked for an interview, do not miss it
SNAP and many other programs require a phone or in-person interview. Missing it usually means denial — and starting over. If you cannot make the time given, call and reschedule immediately. Do not just not show up.
6
Get help if any part of this feels overwhelming
Free application assistance exists in most places. Call 211 and ask for "benefits enrollment help" — they will connect you to a local navigator who will sit with you (in person, by phone, or by video) and walk through the form line by line. This is a free service. Use it.
What to SAY
1
Calling 211 for help applying
"I'm trying to apply for [SNAP / Medicaid / housing assistance / other] and I need help filling out the application. Can you connect me with a navigator or enrollment assister in my area?"
2
If a question on the form confuses you
"I'm not sure how to answer question [number]. Can you explain what it's asking, and what kind of answer they're looking for? I don't want to put down something wrong."
3
During the eligibility interview
"I want to make sure I'm answering this correctly. Can you tell me what you're asking, and I'll give you the most accurate information I have?"
4
If you are denied and don't understand why
"I received a denial notice but I don't understand the reason. Can you walk me through exactly why I was denied, and what my appeal options are? I want to file an appeal."
5
If you feel judged or talked down to
"I'm doing my best to give accurate information. I'd appreciate it if we could go through this step by step." Then keep going. The interaction is about getting the benefit, not about how it feels in the moment.
If you are denied — appeal almost always
You have the right to appeal a denial. The appeal deadline is usually short (10-90 days) — start the appeal even if you do not have all your reasons sorted yet
Legal Aid offices help with benefit denials for free — find one at lsc.gov/find-legal-aid
Many denials are reversed at appeal. The first decision is not the final decision
If you are denied benefits you need to survive (food, medical care, housing), 211 can also connect you to emergency assistance while the appeal is processing
You paid into these systems. Using them is exactly what they exist for.
SNAP, Medicaid, and similar programs exist because everyone in this country agrees, through their taxes, that nobody should have to choose between food and rent, or skip medical care because they cannot afford it. Applying for them is not asking for charity. It is using a system that was built — using money you have contributed to your whole working life — for exactly this kind of moment. The forms are confusing because they were designed by people who do not have to fill them out. The fact that you are pushing through anyway means you are doing it right.

Your First Call to SSA, SSI, or SSDI

You are about to call Social Security — to apply for disability, to start retirement benefits, to claim SSI, or to figure out what you qualify for. The phone tree is long. The hold times can be hours. The questions feel like they have wrong answers. Knowing what to expect makes this call go differently. Most people only call SSA once or twice in their life. Going in prepared changes everything.

Before you call
Set aside 2-3 hours. Have water and a snack. Be near a charger.
Have ready
Social Security number, birth date, address, work history, medical records summary if applying for disability.
Truth
The first answer is not always the final answer. If denied, you can appeal — and most successful claims win on appeal.
What YOU do
1
Decide which benefit you are calling about before you dial
Social Security Retirement (you've worked enough quarters and are old enough). SSDI (you've worked recently and have a disability). SSI (low income with a disability or 65+, regardless of work history). Medicare. If you're not sure, that's okay — the rep will help you figure out — but knowing the words helps.
2
Gather your documents and information
Social Security number. Birth date. Address. Work history (employers, dates). If disability: list of medical conditions, doctors, hospitals you have seen. Bank info if you want direct deposit. Marriage and divorce dates if applicable. Information about your spouse and minor children.
3
Call the main number: 1-800-772-1213
Best times to call: Wednesday, Thursday, or Friday, and later in the day (after 4 p.m.). Avoid Mondays and the first week of the month. Hold times can still be long. Use speaker phone and stay near it.
4
Write down the rep's name and a confirmation number
At the start of the call, ask for the rep's name. At the end, ask for a confirmation or reference number for the call. Write down what was decided and what they said the next step is. Date the notes.
5
Answer honestly, but do not volunteer extra detail
Answer the questions they ask. Truthfully. Briefly. Do not over-explain. If they ask "can you lift 10 pounds?" do not say "well, sometimes, on a good day, if I'm careful…" Just answer the question they asked.
⚠ Especially for disability claims: vague or contradictory answers can be used to deny. Be specific. "I cannot reliably lift 10 pounds without pain" is better than "sometimes I can."
6
If applying for disability, get help
SSDI and SSI disability claims are denied at the first level about 65% of the time. A disability lawyer or advocate can dramatically improve your odds — and they only get paid if you win (capped by federal law). The application process can take months. Patience is part of this.
7
If denied, appeal — almost always
Denials can be appealed and many are reversed. The deadline is usually 60 days. Get a lawyer before the hearing stage. Legal aid handles some cases for free; paid disability lawyers work on contingency.
What to SAY
1
Opening the call
"My name is [Full Name]. My Social Security number is [SSN]. I would like to start an application for [retirement benefits / SSDI / SSI / Medicare]. Before we begin, may I have your name and a reference number for this call?"
2
If you are not sure what you qualify for
"I am not sure which benefit I qualify for. I am [age], I [worked / did not work] for the last [X] years, and I have [a disability / a medical condition / no condition]. Can you help me figure out what to apply for?"
3
When they ask about your condition (disability claims)
"I have [diagnosed condition]. It limits me in the following ways: [specific limitations — be concrete: cannot stand for more than 20 minutes, cannot lift more than 10 pounds, cannot concentrate for more than 30 minutes, etc.]."
4
If they say you do not qualify
"I would like to apply anyway and let the formal decision be made through the application process. Can you start the application?" SSA reps sometimes discourage applications informally. You have the right to officially apply and get a written decision.
5
When ending the call
"Before we hang up — what is the next step, what should I expect to receive in the mail, and by what date? May I have a reference number for this call?"
If you need help with this
Local Social Security office — search "[your city] SSA office" — in-person appointments often easier than phone for first applications
For disability: nonprofit disability advocates (search "disability advocate [your state]") help free or contingency-based
For seniors: Area Agency on Aging (eldercare.acl.gov) helps navigate Social Security, Medicare, and related benefits
For low-income: Legal Aid (lsc.gov/find-legal-aid) helps with SSI applications and appeals
For non-English speakers: SSA provides free interpreters — say "I need an interpreter for [language]" at the start of the call
This system is built to be patient with you. So can you be.
Social Security is one of the most important programs the country has, and it is also one of the slowest and most paperwork-heavy. The wait times, the multiple calls, the appeals — none of it is because you did something wrong. It is built into the system. The people who get what they are entitled to are not the ones who get it right on the first call. They are the ones who keep records, follow up, ask questions, and do not give up when they are told no the first time. You have rights here. You have time. You can call again.

DMV or ID Office When Your Documents Are a Mess

You need an ID. To get the ID, you need other documents. To get those documents, you need more documents. The chain feels designed to fail. For people coming home from incarceration, people who moved here from another country, people who never had clear paperwork to begin with, this loop is real and exhausting. Here is how to break it open, one document at a time.

First thing
You are not going to solve this in one visit. Plan for two or three trips.
Truth
There is almost always a path. The clerks know workarounds they do not volunteer.
Always do
Bring every piece of paper you have, even old ones. Some of them count.
What YOU do
1
Before you go: find the document checklist online
Every state DMV has a list of accepted documents on their website. Search "[your state] DMV ID requirements" — print it or screenshot it. Bring it with you. If a clerk says you need something not on the list, you can point to the list.
2
Bring everything — even old documents you think don't count
Expired ID, old school records, hospital paperwork, jail or prison release papers, parole or probation paperwork, old utility bills, mail with your name and address, court documents, anything with your name on it. Some of these count toward proving identity or residence even when you think they don't.
3
If they say no, ask "what would qualify?"
Do not argue with the first "no." Instead ask what specifically would satisfy the requirement. Often the answer reveals a simpler document you didn't know counted.
⚠ Some clerks are stricter than the rules require. If something on the official list is refused, calmly ask to speak to a supervisor.
4
If you don't have a birth certificate, that's the first thing to get
A birth certificate is the document most other IDs are built on. You can order one from the vital records office in the state where you were born. Cost is usually $20-30. If cost is a barrier, ask the office about a fee waiver — many states have them for low-income applicants.
5
If you don't have proof of address, here are options
A letter from a shelter, transitional housing, halfway house, or social services agency confirming where you live can count. A utility bill in your name is the gold standard, but if you don't have utilities in your name, ask whoever you live with to write a letter — many states accept a notarized statement from a household member.
6
For people coming home from prison or jail
Many states have a reentry ID program that fast-tracks the process. Your parole officer, reentry program, or 211 can connect you. Some states issue an ID before release. Ask whoever is helping you with reentry whether this is set up in your state.
7
Get help from a navigator if the loop is stuck
Reentry programs, immigration legal aid offices, and social service agencies often have staff whose entire job is helping people break out of the documents-loop. Call 211 and ask for a "document navigator" or "ID assistance program" in your area.
What to SAY
1
At the counter, before they say no
"I want to apply for an ID. I have [list what you have]. Can we go through what I have and figure out what's missing, so I know exactly what to bring next time?"
2
If they refuse a document you brought
"I see on your official list that [document type] is accepted. Can you help me understand why this specific one doesn't qualify? Or could a supervisor take a look?"
3
If you're coming home from incarceration
"I was recently released from [facility]. I have my release paperwork and my Social Security card. Does your office have a reentry ID program, or any other path for someone in my situation?"
4
Calling 211 for navigator help
"I'm trying to get an ID and I'm stuck in a documents loop. Can you connect me with someone who helps people navigate this — a document navigator, reentry program, or social services worker?"
5
When you feel like giving up at the counter
Take a slow breath. Then say: "Let me try one more thing. What's the smallest piece I'm missing to make this work today?" If the answer is something you cannot solve today, say: "Okay. I'll come back. Can I have your name so I can ask for you next time?"
When you need extra help
For reentry-specific ID help, the National Reentry Network and many state reentry coalitions have free document assistance
For immigration-related ID issues, your local Legal Aid office or an immigration legal services nonprofit can help (avoid paid "notarios" who are not lawyers)
If you are homeless and need ID to access shelter or services, many continuum-of-care agencies have ID assistance funds
If a clerk treats you with disrespect, you have the right to ask for a supervisor or file a complaint — agency complaint lines are usually posted on their website
The loop is not your fault. Breaking it takes patience, not perfection.
The reason this feels impossible is because the system was designed for people who already have stable paperwork — not for people rebuilding from a hard moment. Coming home from incarceration, moving here from another country, surviving a fire or a move or an evacuation that destroyed your documents — none of these mean you do not deserve an ID. They mean the system has to work harder for you than it does for someone who never lost anything. You are not failing. You are pushing through a system that was not built with you in mind. Every step forward, even a small one, counts.
Family · Parenting

Talking to Your Kid About Something Hard

A divorce. A diagnosis. A job loss. A death. A move. A relapse. Something happened — or is about to — and you have to tell your child. You are scared of saying the wrong thing, of making it worse, of seeing their face when they hear it. You are not going to do this perfectly. No one does. But there are ways to do it that protect them, and you, in a moment that already feels like it is too much.

Truth
Kids handle hard news better when they hear it from someone who loves them, in their own home, in plain words.
Permission
You are allowed to feel scared, sad, or unsure while you do this. You do not have to be okay to be a good parent in this moment.
Remember
This is not one conversation. It is the first of many. You do not have to cover everything today.
What YOU do — before the talk
1
Take care of yourself first, even just a little
Before you sit them down, take 10 minutes alone. Cry if you need to. Splash water on your face. Call one person who knows. You do not need to be calm. You need to be steady enough to start.
2
Pick the right time and place
Not bedtime — they need time to process before sleep. Not at school drop-off. Somewhere safe and familiar — at home, at the kitchen table, on the couch. With enough time afterward to be together. A weekend morning often works best.
3
Match the words to their age — and use the real word for the thing
Younger kids need short sentences and concrete facts. Older kids can handle more. But use the actual word: "cancer," "divorce," "died." Soft phrases ("passed away," "things will be different") confuse younger children. They need the real word and then the explanation.
4
Lead with the facts they need, not the emotion
"Mommy and Daddy are getting a divorce. That means we are going to live in two different houses. You will still see both of us. None of this is because of anything you did." Cover the basics first: what happened, what changes, what stays the same, that they are loved and safe.
5
Tell them what is NOT changing
Kids fixate on the change. Reassure them about what stays: who loves them, where they sleep, who picks them up, that they will still see [people they care about]. Even if some of those things are changing, name the things that are not.
6
Make space for their reaction — whatever it is
They may cry. They may be silent. They may say "okay" and ask to play video games. They may get angry at you. All of these are normal. There is no wrong way for them to react. Do not push them to "have feelings about it" if they don't want to talk yet.
7
Tell them they can ask anything, anytime
Do not promise to have all the answers. Tell them: "You can ask me anything. If I do not know, I will tell you I do not know. If I cannot tell you yet, I will tell you that too. We are going to figure this out together."
8
Loop in the other adults around them
Tell their teacher, daycare, coach, or other caregivers what is going on so they can watch for signs and respond well if your kid acts out, withdraws, or asks questions. This is not gossip — this is protecting your child's circle.
What to SAY
1
Opening the conversation
"I need to talk to you about something important. It is hard news, and I want to tell you myself before you hear it from anyone else. You are not in trouble. Come sit with me."
2
Telling the news, plainly
"[The thing] happened. That means [what it means for them — concrete change]. Here is what is NOT changing: [list of stable things]. Here is what I know right now, and here is what I do not know yet."
3
If they ask "is it my fault?"
"No. This is not because of anything you did, anything you said, or anything you thought. This is between grown-ups, and you did not cause it. I want you to hear me say that as many times as you need to."
4
If they ask a hard question you cannot answer
"That is a really good question, and I do not know the answer right now. I promise I will tell you as soon as I do. It is okay to ask me again later."
5
If you start to cry mid-conversation
"I am crying because this is hard and I love you. It is okay for both of us to feel sad about this. I am still your [mom/dad/parent] and I am still going to take care of you."
6
Closing the conversation
"You can ask me anything, anytime. Even tonight. Even tomorrow. Even in a year. We are going to keep talking about this. You are loved. We are going to be okay."
If you need help thinking about this conversation
For divorce or separation: Sesame Workshop's "Helping Kids Cope With Divorce" has free, age-appropriate materials (sesamestreet.org/divorce)
For serious illness or death: The Dougy Center (dougy.org) has free guides for grieving kids by age group
For deployment or military separation: Sesame Workshop's Military Families program has free resources for explaining deployment
For parental incarceration: Sesame Street's "Little Children, Big Challenges" toolkit covers explaining incarceration to young kids
For parental disability or chronic illness: Family Caregiver Alliance has materials on how to talk to kids about a parent's diagnosis
If your child shows persistent signs of distress (changes in sleep, eating, school, withdrawal), a child therapist can help — your pediatrician can refer you
You are not going to do this perfectly. You are going to do it lovingly. That is what they will remember.
Years from now, your child will not remember the exact words you used. They will remember that you sat down with them. That you told them yourself. That you cried with them or held them or just sat in the quiet. That you said it was not their fault. That you came back the next day to talk again. That is the whole thing. That is what good parenting in a hard moment looks like — not having the right script, but being there, being honest, and coming back. You are doing it right by trying this hard.
Family · Telling your parent

Telling a Parent Something Hard

A diagnosis. A divorce. A loss of a job. A relapse. A pregnancy they did not expect. A sexuality you have not named to them. A debt you got into. A truth about something that happened to you when you were younger. Something your kid is going through. You are an adult now, and you have to tell a parent something that is going to land hard. You do not owe them every detail. But you want — or need — to tell them. Here is how to do that without it costing you more than it has to.

Truth
You are an adult. You get to decide what you share, how much, and when.
Permission
You do not have to manage their reaction. Their feelings are theirs to handle.
Expect
Their first reaction is rarely their final reaction. Give the conversation room to evolve.
Before the conversation
1
Decide why you are telling them
Are you telling them because they need to know? Because you want their support? Because you are tired of hiding it? Because they are going to find out anyway and you want them to hear it from you? Knowing your reason shapes what you say and helps you stay steady if they react badly.
2
Decide what you ARE and ARE NOT sharing
You do not have to tell them everything. You are an adult. You can say "I want to tell you the headline; the details are between me and [doctor / lawyer / therapist / partner]." Knowing in advance what is on the table and what is not protects you from oversharing when emotions get high.
3
Pick the right setting
Some news is better in person. Some is better on the phone where you have control over the call. Some is better in a letter or message where they can read it once, sit with it, and come back. Match the format to how this parent processes things — and to how safe you feel being in the room with them when they hear it.
4
Have an exit plan
If they react in a way that hurts you, where will you go? Who will you call? Plan this before the conversation, not during it. If you are at their home, have a way to leave. If you are on the phone, give yourself permission to end the call.
During the conversation
5
Lead with the news. Do not bury it.
Long preambles make it worse. "Mom, Dad — I have something to tell you. [The thing]." Then stop and let them respond. Do not over-explain in the first thirty seconds. Give them room to absorb it.
6
Let their first reaction be their first reaction
They may cry. They may be angry. They may say something hurtful they later regret. They may say nothing. None of these are the final word on how they feel. Many parents say the wrong thing in the first ten minutes and the right thing in the next ten days.
⚠ Resist the urge to take back the news or apologize for telling them. You did the right thing by telling them.
7
If they push for details you do not want to share, hold the line
"I am sharing what I am sharing. The rest is private." Repeat as needed. You can love them and still not tell them everything.
8
End the conversation when it is enough
You do not have to stay until they are okay with it. You can say "I love you, I told you what I needed to tell you, and we can talk more when we both have had time." Then end the conversation.
9
Take care of yourself afterward
Whatever happened, telling a parent something hard is a heavy thing. Call a friend. Eat something. Sleep if you can. Do not make any other big decisions today.
What to SAY
1
Opening the conversation
"I want to tell you something. I have been thinking about when to share this, and now feels like the time. [The thing]. I am telling you because [your reason]. I do not need you to fix anything — I just wanted you to know."
2
When they react badly in the moment
"I hear that this is hard for you. It is hard for me too. I am not asking you to be okay with this right now. I am just asking you to listen."
3
If they push for details you do not want to share
"I am sharing what I am ready to share. The rest is private for now. I might tell you more later, but I might not — and that has to be okay."
4
If they say something hurtful
"What you just said hurt. I'm going to end this conversation for now and come back to it when we have both had time to think. I love you."
5
If they say nothing
"I know this is a lot. You don't have to say anything right now. Take whatever time you need. I am here when you want to talk."
6
Coming back days later if needed
"I want to follow up on what I told you. I am not asking you to change how you feel — I just want us to be able to talk about it as it goes. Is now a good time, or should we pick a time?"
If this conversation could go a hard direction
If you are telling a parent something where their reaction could put you at risk (housing, safety, financial dependence), think carefully about timing and setting. A therapist can help you plan a high-stakes disclosure
For LGBTQ+ adults disclosing identity to family: The Trevor Project (thetrevorproject.org) and PFLAG (pflag.org) have resources and support for navigating family disclosure at any age
For people in recovery telling family about relapse or treatment: SAMHSA (1-800-662-4357) has resources for family conversations about substance use
For adult survivors disclosing childhood abuse or trauma to a parent: RAINN (1-800-656-4673) has trained counselors who can help you prepare
For first-generation adults disclosing in cultural-family contexts: identity-specific therapy directories (Asian Mental Health Collective, Therapy for Black Girls, Latinx Therapy, and similar) connect you with therapists who understand the context
If the conversation reveals or risks abuse from a parent, the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233) supports adult children too — not just spouses
You are not asking permission. You are sharing your life.
You spent a long time as a child whose news your parents got to react to as they pleased. You are not that child anymore. You are an adult sharing news from your adult life — and how they respond is not the measure of whether you did the right thing in telling them. You did the right thing by deciding what to share, choosing a moment, saying the words, and letting them have their reaction. The conversation does not end today. Their first response is not their final one. You did something brave. Whatever they do with it is their work, not yours.
Relationships · Boundaries

Setting a Boundary with Someone Who Won't Accept It

You have told them. Maybe more than once. A parent, a friend, a partner, a coworker, a neighbor, a sibling, a family member, someone from your church or community — and they keep stepping over the line you asked them to respect. They tell you you're being dramatic, overreacting, selfish, or that "this isn't a big deal." You are not wrong to ask for what you asked for. Holding the boundary without exploding the relationship is a separate skill — and it is the same skill, whoever they are.

Truth
A boundary is a statement about what YOU will do. It is not a request for their permission.
Permission
You are allowed to set limits in any relationship — close, casual, family, professional, or community.
Expect
They may not respond well at first. That does not mean the boundary is wrong.
What YOU do — get clear first
1
Get clear, alone, on what the boundary actually is
Vague boundaries get crossed. "Stop being so critical" is a feeling. "I will end the call if you criticize my parenting" is a boundary. Write down: what behavior, and what you will do if it happens. Specific. Whether the person is your mother, your best friend, your coworker, or your neighbor — the work of being specific is the same.
2
Decide the consequence in advance — and be willing to follow through
A boundary without follow-through is a suggestion. If the consequence is "I will leave the room," you have to be willing to leave. If it is "I will end the call," you have to be willing to hang up. Pick a consequence you can actually do. Smaller consequences you will keep are better than big consequences you will not.
3
Pick a calm moment to state it — not mid-conflict
Boundaries set during a fight almost never hold. Wait until things are calm. Ask for a private moment if needed. Then say it once, clearly, without a long defense or apology.
4
Hold it without defending it
If they argue, get hurt, or try to negotiate, you do not have to defend the boundary point by point. Repeat it. "I understand you feel that way. The boundary is still the same."
⚠ Defending the boundary turns it into a debate. The boundary is not up for debate. It is a statement of what you will do.
5
Follow through the first time it is crossed
The first crossing is the test. If you follow through that first time — leave, end the call, end the visit, stop responding for the rest of the day — the boundary becomes real. If you do not, you have just taught them this boundary is negotiable.
6
Expect a hard week (or longer). Stay steady anyway.
A person who has not respected boundaries before will often escalate when you set one — anger, guilt-trips, silent treatment, going through other people in your life to pressure you. This is the part most people give up at. The other side of this hard stretch is a calmer relationship — or a clearer one. Both are valid outcomes.
7
Get support outside the relationship in question
A therapist, a counselor, a trusted friend, or even a peer support group can help you hold the boundary when guilt and outside pressure mount. Doing this alone is much harder than doing it with someone in your corner.
What to SAY
1
Stating the boundary
"I want to talk about something important. When [specific behavior happens], it [specific impact on you]. Going forward, if [behavior] happens, I will [specific consequence]. I am telling you this because I want our relationship to work, and I need this to change."
2
When they argue or get hurt
"I hear that this is hard for you. The boundary is still the same. I am not changing my mind because you are upset about it."
3
When they accuse you of overreacting or being selfish
"I am setting a limit, not a verdict on you. I care about this relationship. I am also going to do this. Both are true."
4
Following through the first time
"I told you what I was going to do if this happened. I am doing it now. We can talk again when [time / condition]." Then do the thing. Hang up. Leave. End the visit. Stop replying for the day.
5
When other people in your life get pulled into it
"This is between me and [person]. I'm not going to relitigate it through other people. If you want to support me, you can tell them to come to me directly."
6
If it is a coworker or professional context
"I want to keep working together well. Going forward, I need [specific thing]. If [behavior] happens again, I am going to [specific work-appropriate consequence — talk to HR, decline that kind of task, end the meeting, etc.]." Be specific and professional. Document.
If the relationship is more than just difficult
If anyone in your life is verbally, emotionally, or physically abusive, what you are dealing with is more than boundaries — please consider speaking to a therapist or calling the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 for guidance
For workplace boundaries that involve harassment or discrimination, the EEOC (eeoc.gov) handles formal complaints, and most workplaces have HR escalation paths
For low-cost therapy support to help hold boundaries: Open Path Collective (openpathcollective.org) and Federally Qualified Health Centers offer affordable mental health care
For first-generation adults navigating cultural family expectations, communities like Asian Mental Health Collective, Therapy for Black Girls, Latinx Therapy, and similar can connect you with therapists who understand the context
If holding a boundary in a relationship has become impossible without consequences to your kids, job, or housing, a counselor can help you think through next steps
Boundaries are an act of care — for them, for you, for the relationship.
It does not feel that way when you are doing it. It feels like betrayal, like you are the bad person, like you are breaking something good. But a relationship without boundaries is not a closer relationship — it is one where one person disappears so the other can stay comfortable. Setting limits does not mean you care less. It means you are trying to build a version of the relationship that you can actually sustain for the long term, instead of the version that is burning you out right now. Whoever the person is — parent, partner, friend, family member, coworker, neighbor — the principle holds. That is grown-up care. It is the hardest kind.
Relationships · Asking and answering

Someone You Love Asks for Help You Can't Give

A family member needs money you do not have. A friend is asking you to co-sign a loan. A sibling needs a place to stay and you cannot host them right now. A partner wants something from you you cannot give. The ask is real, your love for them is real, and your no is also real. Saying no without losing the relationship is a specific skill — different from setting an ongoing boundary, because this is one moment, one ask, one answer. Here is how to give the answer without giving up the relationship.

Truth
No is a complete answer. You do not owe a detailed reason for it.
Permission
Saying no to one ask does not mean you love them less. It means you know what you can actually do.
Remember
Saying yes when you cannot afford to causes real damage — to your finances, your stability, and the relationship itself.
What YOU do
1
Do not answer in the moment if you do not have to
"Let me think about it and get back to you" is one of the most underused sentences in adult life. Asks in the moment often feel urgent when they are not. Give yourself a few hours or a day to check what you can actually afford — emotionally, financially, time-wise — before committing.
2
Be honest with yourself about your capacity
Ask: Can I actually do this without putting myself in a hard spot? Will I resent doing it? Will it hurt my own family, my own bills, my own health? If the answer to any of those is yes, the answer to the ask is probably no — and that is okay.
3
Say no clearly — not maybe, not vaguely
"Maybe" or "I'll see what I can do" when you actually mean no leaves them hoping and you avoiding. It hurts the relationship more than a clean no. Give them the answer they need to plan around.
⚠ A clear no respects them more than a vague maybe. They can move on and find another path. A maybe leaves them stuck.
4
Do not over-explain
You do not need to list every reason. Long explanations sound like negotiations — like you are looking for permission. A short, honest reason is enough. "I can't do this right now" is a complete sentence.
5
If you can do something smaller, offer that — but only if you actually can
"I can't give you the $500, but I can give you $50" — only if $50 is actually fine for you. Do not offer a smaller version out of guilt that you will also regret. A real smaller offer can preserve the relationship. A guilt-driven one builds resentment.
6
If you can help in a non-money way, name it
"I can't lend you the money, but I can help you look into [program / resource / option]." Pointing them toward resources is real help — sometimes more useful than the cash they asked for. 211 is often a good first call for someone needing housing, food, utility, or financial help.
7
Hold the no if they push back
If they get hurt, angry, or guilt-trip you, the answer is still no. You can sit with their disappointment without changing your mind. Their pain is not a sign you should change your answer — it is a sign they are also dealing with a hard situation.
8
Stay in the relationship after the no
A no is not a goodbye. Check in with them in a few days. Send a regular message. Show up to dinner. The relationship continues. The no was about this one ask, not about them as a person.
What to SAY
1
Buying yourself time
"That's a big ask and I want to give you a real answer, not a quick one. Let me think about it and get back to you by [day/time]."
2
The clear no
"I thought about it carefully, and I'm not able to do this. I love you and I wish the answer could be different — but I can't."
3
When they ask why
"I just don't have the [money / room / capacity] to do it right now without putting myself in a hard spot. I know that's not the answer you were hoping for."
4
Offering a smaller real version (if you actually can)
"I can't do [the whole ask], but here is what I can do: [smaller real offer]. I want to help where I actually can."
5
Pointing toward another resource
"I can't help with this directly, but I want to help you figure out a path. Have you tried [211 / local agency / specific program]? I can sit with you while you make the call if that would help."
6
When they push back hard
"I hear that this is hard, and I am sorry I can't help in the way you were hoping. My answer is the same. I love you and I am not going back on this."
7
Staying in the relationship afterward
A few days later: "Hey — just thinking about you. How are things going? I wanted to check in." A no does not have to be followed by silence. Keep showing up in normal ways.
If the ask is a pattern or the person is in real crisis
If a family member or friend keeps asking for money in a way that is straining you, that is a different situation — see the "Setting a Boundary" guide. One ask is a moment. A pattern needs a different conversation.
If the person asking is in real crisis (homelessness, eviction, food insecurity, medical emergency), 211 (call or text) connects them to local emergency resources at no cost
For someone facing eviction: see the "Eviction notice arrived" guide — there are emergency rental assistance programs specifically for this
For a friend or family member in mental health crisis: 988 (call or text) is the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. SAMHSA helpline (1-800-662-4357) handles non-crisis mental health and substance use
If someone is repeatedly asking for money in a way that might be related to addiction or financial exploitation, Al-Anon (al-anon.org) and similar support groups help family members navigate this
If you are being asked to co-sign a loan or take on financial risk for someone else, talk to a financial counselor first — nfcc.org connects you to free nonprofit credit counseling
Saying no is not failing them. Saying yes you cannot deliver on is.
The most damaging thing in relationships built around asks is not the no. It is the yes that becomes a no later — the help that was promised but could not be sustained, the loan that was made but resented, the room that was offered but came with bitterness. A clean no, said with care, leaves the relationship intact. A yes you cannot follow through on hollows it out. You are allowed to know what you can do. You are allowed to say it. The people who love you will adjust. The ones who do not are showing you something — and that is information, not a loss.
Workplace · Job loss

You Just Got Fired or Laid Off

It just happened — or it happened today, or yesterday. Your stomach dropped. You may have signed something, you may have walked out with a box, you may have hung up the phone in shock. Right now, the most important thing is not to make a decision you can't take back. There is real work to do, and it can wait one day. Before anything else, give yourself one day to land.

Right now
Do not sign anything else today. Do not call anyone in anger. Do not announce it on social media.
Truth
Losing a job — even being fired — does not define you. Many of the most respected people in your life have been through this.
First call tomorrow
Your state's unemployment office. Always apply, even if you think you might not qualify.
Today — buffer first
1
Do not sign anything they hand you today
Severance agreements, releases, non-disparagement clauses — these are often presented in the room or attached to a termination letter. You have time to read them. Most are negotiable. Do not sign on the spot, even if you feel pressure to. Take it home, read it, sleep on it.
🛑 Never sign a severance or release on the same day you are terminated. You have the right to take it with you.
2
Save every email, text, and document right now
If you still have access to your work email, forward important emails to your personal email — performance reviews, schedules, anything related to the firing, any praise you received. Take photos of paper documents. If your access is already cut off, write down what you remember.
3
Write down what just happened while it is fresh
Date, time, who said what, what reason was given (if any), what was promised about pay, benefits, or final check. Do this in your own private notes — not on company systems. You may need this later, even if you think you won't.
4
Tell one person you trust — only one — today
Not your whole network. Not social media. One person. A family member, a close friend, a mentor. You need to say it out loud to someone, but you do not need to manage other people's reactions tonight.
5
Eat something. Drink water. Sleep if you can.
Job loss is a shock to your body, not just your mind. Treat it like recovering from any other shock. The real work starts tomorrow.
Tomorrow — start the work
6
File for unemployment — even if you think you don't qualify
Search "[your state] unemployment apply." File the same day you can. Even if you were fired (not laid off), file anyway. Eligibility is decided by the state, not by your former employer. Many fired workers qualify, especially if the firing was for performance issues rather than misconduct.
7
Look up your health insurance options
COBRA lets you keep your employer's plan but is expensive. The Marketplace (healthcare.gov) often has cheaper options — and job loss qualifies you for a Special Enrollment Period (60 days from loss of coverage). Medicaid is also worth checking. Do not go uninsured.
8
Read any severance papers carefully — get advice if anything feels off
Severance is often negotiable. If you were fired in a way that feels unfair (especially if it followed a complaint, a request for accommodation, or anything that might be discrimination), it is worth a free consultation with an employment lawyer before signing. Many lawyers offer free initial calls.
What to SAY
1
If they hand you papers in the room
"I want to make sure I understand this fully before I sign anything. I'm going to take this home, read it carefully, and get back to you. When do you need a response?"
2
If they pressure you to sign on the spot
"I understand you'd like a quick response. I'm not in a position to sign something this important without reading it carefully. I'll get back to you by [day]."
3
Telling the one person you trust
"I lost my job today. I'm not ready to talk through what happens next — I just needed to tell someone."
4
Calling the unemployment office
"I lost my job on [date] and I'm calling to file an unemployment claim. I'm not sure if I qualify, but I want to apply and let the state decide."
5
When the voice in your head says you're failing
"I am between things. That is not the same as failing. People I respect have been here. I will move through this too."
If something about the firing feels wrong
If you were fired after reporting harassment, requesting an accommodation, taking medical leave, getting pregnant, or in any way that feels like retaliation — consult an employment lawyer before signing anything. Many offer free consultations
EEOC (eeoc.gov) handles workplace discrimination complaints. There is a filing deadline (usually 180-300 days)
If you were not paid for your final hours, your last paycheck is owed and protected by state wage law. Your state labor department handles wage complaints
If you are losing health insurance and have an ongoing medical need, call your doctor's office immediately — they can sometimes provide medication or care bridges
A job ending is a chapter ending, not your story ending.
Right now, every fear feels permanent. The shame, the worry, the not-knowing what comes next — all of it feels like it will last forever. It will not. Almost everyone you respect has been through a job loss at some point in their working life. They survived it. You will too. The work tomorrow is just the next step, not the final answer. For today, your only job is to be gentle with yourself, protect what you have, and not make any decision you cannot take back. The rest can wait until you have slept.
Workplace · Returning to work

Job Interview After a Long Gap

It has been a while. Maybe you were home with kids. Maybe you were caring for a parent. Maybe you were in treatment, in prison, in recovery, dealing with your health, deployed, or just rebuilding after life knocked you sideways. Whatever it was — you are sitting down for an interview now, and you are worried they are going to ask about the gap. They will. Here is how to handle it without shame and without overexplaining.

Truth
A resume gap is not a red flag. Hiring managers see them constantly. What matters is how you talk about it.
Frame
Brief. Honest. Forward. Brief about what you did, honest about why, forward to what you bring now.
Never
Apologize for the gap. You are not asking for forgiveness. You are applying for a job.
What YOU do — before the interview
1
Write your gap explanation in 2-3 sentences — and practice saying it out loud
Not a long story. Not a justification. A short, steady answer: what you were doing, why it ended, what you are ready for now. Practice in the mirror or to a friend until the words come out without your voice shaking.
2
Decide what you are comfortable sharing — and what you are not
You do not owe a complete medical history, a story of your incarceration, the details of a divorce, or the truth about a relapse. Decide before the interview what is on the table and what is not. You can say "personal health reasons" or "a family situation" without specifics.
3
Make a list of what you actually did during the gap
Even things that do not feel like "work" count. Took care of a parent. Managed a household budget. Learned a skill. Volunteered. Recovered from something hard. These are real and they built things in you. Pick one or two to mention if the conversation goes there.
4
Prepare your "what I bring now" line
After the gap explanation, pivot to what you bring to the role. "What that time taught me…" or "Coming back, I am bringing a kind of focus I did not have before…" — something true that turns the page forward.
In the interview
5
Answer the question once. Move on.
When they ask about the gap, give your prepared answer. Do not add more if they do not ask. Do not loop back to it later in the interview. Answer once, pivot to what you bring, move forward.
⚠ Overexplaining is the most common mistake. It signals shame. Brief and steady signals confidence.
6
If they push for more, you can decline politely
"I'd rather keep the details private — but what I can tell you is [the relevant part]." Most interviewers will respect this. The ones who do not are showing you something about the workplace.
7
If a background check is involved (reentry context)
If your gap was incarceration and a background check is coming, you generally do better naming it briefly before they find it themselves. "I have a record from [time period]. I have done [what you did since]. I am happy to talk about it if you have questions." Many employers are more open than people fear, especially in states with fair-chance hiring laws.
8
Ask your own questions
Coming back from a gap, you are also interviewing them. Ask about training and onboarding. Ask about flexibility if you need it. Ask what success looks like in the first 90 days. This signals confidence and helps you decide if this is the right place.
What to SAY
1
When they ask "Tell us about your gap"
"For [X time], I was [brief, honest reason — caring for a parent / focused on my health / serving / raising my kids / completing a sentence / supporting my family through a transition]. That chapter is closed and I am ready to be back in the workforce. What I bring to this role is [strength]."
2
If they ask for details you do not want to share
"I'd prefer to keep the personal details private — what I can tell you is that the situation is resolved, I am fully ready to focus on work, and I am bringing [what you bring] to this role."
3
For reentry: naming a record yourself
"You will see in the background check that I have a record from [year range]. I served my time, completed [program / probation / etc.], and have been [working / volunteering / building] since. I am happy to answer any questions you have."
4
If they ask about caregiving / disability accommodations
"I have what I need to do this job. If anything comes up where an adjustment would help, I will let you know — but I am here ready to do the work." (You are not required to disclose disability in an interview; you can decide what to share.)
5
Closing the interview
"Thank you for the conversation. I am very interested in this role. What are the next steps, and when can I expect to hear back?"
Resources for specific situations
For reentry: Honest Jobs (honestjobs.com) and 70MillionJobs are platforms for fair-chance employers
For veterans: Hire Heroes USA (hireheroesusa.org) provides free career coaching to veterans and military spouses
For people returning after disability: Job Accommodation Network (askjan.org) provides free guidance on accommodations and disability disclosure
For caregivers returning to work: Path Forward and iRelaunch run "returnship" programs for people coming back after a caregiving gap
For interview practice: most American Job Centers (CareerOneStop.org) offer free mock interview coaching
If an employer rejects you specifically because of your gap or your record in a state with fair-chance laws, you may have legal recourse — check with your state attorney general's office
You did not waste those years. You lived them. Now you are showing up to work.
Whatever you were doing during the gap — surviving, caregiving, healing, serving, raising — was real life. It built things in you that you may not even see yet: patience, perspective, the ability to handle hard things calmly, knowing what actually matters. The best employees often come from the people who took the long way back. The interview is the door, not the verdict. You belong in that room. Walk in steady.

Telehealth Visit When Your Internet or Setup Isn't Reliable

You have a telehealth appointment coming up. Your internet drops sometimes. You are not sure if your phone or laptop will work. The link they sent might not load. The whole thing feels designed for people with better setups than you have. Knowing how to handle the technology — and what your alternatives are if it fails — protects your care.

Right
A telehealth visit by phone (no video) is a valid medical visit. You can ask for a phone-only appointment.
Before
Test your setup the day BEFORE, not 5 minutes before the visit.
If it fails
You have the right to be seen another way. Call the office immediately.
What YOU do — the day before
1
Test the link they sent you
Click the link in the email or text. See if it loads. See if it asks you to download an app. Do this the day before — not 5 minutes before the visit. If it does not work, you have time to call the office.
2
Make sure your device is charged and somewhere with the strongest signal
If you are on a phone: full charge, plugged in if possible. If on Wi-Fi: be as close to the router as you can. If your home internet is weak, sit on the porch, by a window, or wherever the signal is strongest. Rural users — sometimes the parked car closest to the road has the best signal.
3
Have a backup plan ready
If the video does not work, you need to know: the office's phone number (write it down on paper), whether they can do the visit by phone, and whether you can reschedule for in-person if the technology fails completely.
4
Call the office and ask for a phone-only option in advance if you want one
You do not have to do video. Telehealth-by-phone is a real medical visit and your insurance probably covers it the same way. Call the office and say you would like a phone visit instead — most will accommodate.
Day of the visit
5
Log on 10 minutes early
Give yourself time. Many telehealth platforms put you in a virtual waiting room. If something is not working — link broken, audio off, video frozen — you have a few minutes to call the office and switch to phone before the visit time.
6
Have your notes ready, just like an in-person visit
List of medications. List of concerns (top three). Any new symptoms. Questions you want answered. Have a pen and paper to write down what the doctor says — it is easier to forget on a screen than in person.
7
If the video freezes or drops, call the office right away
Do not just sit there hoping it comes back. Call the office on a regular phone. They can either restart the visit, switch you to phone, or reschedule. You are not being difficult — connection problems are common and the office knows it.
8
After the visit, request a written summary
Telehealth visits feel less concrete than in-person ones. Ask the office to send a visit summary through the patient portal or by mail. This way you have what the doctor said in writing — medications, follow-up steps, anything they want you to do.
What to SAY
1
Calling to set up a phone-only visit
"My internet is not reliable enough for a video visit. Can my appointment with Dr. [Name] be done by phone instead? I want to make sure the visit can actually happen."
2
When you first log on
"Hi, can you confirm you can see and hear me okay? I want to flag that my internet is not the best — if anything drops, I will call the office line." (Then have that number ready.)
3
If the connection is breaking up mid-visit
"Doctor, I am losing you. Can we switch to a phone call? Let me hang up and try calling the office directly so we can finish the visit."
4
If the visit fails completely
"The connection didn't work today. Can we reschedule, ideally as a phone visit or in-person? I don't want to wait — I came here today for a reason."
5
Ending the visit
"Before we wrap up — can you send me a written summary of what we discussed and any next steps? I want to make sure I have it in writing."
If telehealth is not working for your situation
Federal law (Section 1557 of the ACA) requires healthcare providers to accommodate people who cannot reliably use telehealth — including by offering in-person or phone-only options
Rural Health Information Hub (ruralhealthinfo.org) lists rural-specific health resources, including telehealth alternatives
Federally Qualified Health Centers (findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov) often have community computers and staff who can help patients connect to telehealth visits
Some libraries offer private rooms with reliable internet for telehealth visits — call ahead to ask
For low-cost or free home internet: the FCC's Lifeline program and Affordable Connectivity Program (if active) help low-income households get connected
For seniors: many Area Agencies on Aging (eldercare.acl.gov) provide help with telehealth technology setup
The technology is supposed to serve your care, not the other way around.
Telehealth is supposed to make healthcare more accessible — not less. When the technology fails, the failure is in the system, not in you. You do not need to be ashamed of weak internet, an old phone, or not knowing how to fix the camera. Your doctor's office handles this every day. Phone-only visits are real visits. In-person is still an option. The medical care you need is what matters. The platform is just the door to it — and there is always another door.

A Background Check Returned Wrong Information About You

You applied for a job, an apartment, or a license. The decision came back negative — and when you found out why, the background check has someone else's record on it. Or a charge you were not convicted of. Or something from twenty years ago that should have been sealed. Or a date that is wrong. You are not stuck with this. Federal law gives you specific rights to dispute and correct it.

Right
Under federal law, you can demand a copy of the background check and dispute anything wrong on it — for free.
Timeline
Disputes must be investigated within 30 days. Most errors are correctable.
First step
Get a copy of the report. You cannot fight what you cannot see.
What YOU do
1
Ask the employer or landlord for the "adverse action" notice
If a job or housing decision was made against you because of a background check, federal law (the Fair Credit Reporting Act) requires they give you a copy of the report and the name of the company that produced it. Ask for the adverse action notice in writing.
2
Get your own copy of the background check directly
Once you know the background check company (Sterling, Checkr, HireRight, GoodHire, etc.), contact them and request your file. You are entitled to a free copy. Their contact info is required to be on the adverse action notice.
3
Identify exactly what is wrong
Read the report carefully. Mark each error: wrong name, wrong date of birth, someone else's record listed, charges that were dismissed or expunged, a conviction that should have been sealed, an outdated entry, an incorrect employment record. Be specific about each one.
4
File a dispute in writing with the background check company
Send a written dispute (email or mail with tracking) listing each error and including any proof you have — court records showing dismissal, expungement orders, ID showing the correct date of birth, anything. Keep a copy of everything. The company has 30 days to investigate.
5
Also dispute with the source if it is wrong at the source
If the wrong information is coming from a court record, you may need to also contact the court to correct it there. If it is an expunged record that should not appear, the expungement order needs to be on file. Background check companies can only fix what they pulled — if the source is wrong, the source needs fixing too.
6
After it is corrected, ask for the corrected report to be sent to the employer/landlord
Federal law allows you to request the corrected report be sent to anyone who received the incorrect one within the last two years. Use this. The employer or landlord that turned you down may reconsider once they have accurate information.
7
If the dispute is denied or ignored, escalate
File a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (consumerfinance.gov). They take FCRA complaints seriously and often get results when the company will not. You can also consult a consumer rights attorney — many handle these on contingency.
What to SAY
1
To the employer or landlord
"I understand the decision was based on a background check. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, I am requesting a copy of the report and the name of the company that provided it. I believe there is incorrect information that I need to dispute."
2
To the background check company (in your dispute)
"I am disputing the following items in my background check report dated [date]: [list each item, what is wrong, and what the correct information should be]. I have attached documentation supporting my dispute. Under the FCRA, please investigate and respond within 30 days."
3
If the record is someone else's (identity mix-up)
"This record is not mine. The person on this record has [different name / different date of birth / different Social Security number]. My correct information is [yours]. Please remove this record from my file immediately."
4
After correction, going back to the employer
"The background check has been corrected, and the updated report has been sent to you. The information that affected your decision was inaccurate. I am writing to ask that you reconsider my application with the correct information in hand."
5
If the company stalls or denies the dispute
"I am filing a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and consulting an attorney about FCRA violations. Please be advised that I am preserving all records of this dispute."
Your federal rights and where to escalate
Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (consumerfinance.gov/complaint) handles FCRA complaints — file a complaint if disputes are ignored
National Association of Consumer Advocates (consumeradvocates.org) helps people find attorneys who handle FCRA cases, often on contingency
For sealed or expunged records appearing on checks: your local Legal Aid (lsc.gov/find-legal-aid) can help enforce expungement orders
For reentry: the National Employment Law Project (nelp.org) has resources on background check rights for people with records
For immigrants: if a background check is being used in a way that may discriminate based on national origin, contact the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (eeoc.gov)
Some states have stronger protections than federal law — your state attorney general's office may also accept complaints
Wrong information on a background check is not a final answer. It is a problem with a process to fix it.
Background check errors are common — wrong identities, outdated records, charges that were dropped, expungements that did not get applied, mix-ups between people with the same name. The law was written precisely because these errors keep people out of jobs and housing they should have access to. Fixing it takes paperwork, persistence, and time — but it is fixable. The fact that one report blocked you from one job or apartment does not mean every future report will. You can clean this up. People do, all the time. You have the law on your side.

An Eviction Notice Just Arrived

You opened the mail, or a paper was taped to your door, or someone handed it to you. Your stomach is tight. The first thing to know is this: an eviction notice is not the same as being evicted. There is a process, there is time, and there are protections you have right now. The most important thing you can do is not ignore it and not move out yet.

Right now
Do not move out. Do not throw the notice away. Do not ignore it.
Today or tomorrow
Call legal aid. This is the single most important call you can make.
Truth
A notice is the start of a process, not the end. Tenants who get legal help win or stay much more often than tenants without it.
What YOU do
1
Read the notice carefully and write down key dates
The notice will say what kind it is (pay-or-quit, cure-or-quit, no-cause), how many days you have, and what the landlord claims you owe or did. Write down the date you received it and the deadline given. These dates control everything that happens next.
🛑 Do not throw the notice away. You will need it. Take a photo of it now in case anything happens to the paper copy.
2
Call legal aid today — this is the single most important step
Tenants with legal representation win or stay in their homes far more often than tenants without it. Legal aid is free for people who qualify (usually low income). Find your local office at lsc.gov/find-legal-aid. If they cannot help directly, they will refer you to someone who can.
3
Do not move out unless a judge has ordered it
A notice is not an order to leave. Only a judge, through a court process, can legally make you leave. Leaving voluntarily before that gives up rights you may have. Stay until you have legal advice on what to do.
🛑 If your landlord tries to lock you out, turn off your utilities, or remove your belongings without going through court, that is illegal in every state. Call legal aid or the police immediately.
4
If the notice says you owe rent, gather what you have
Receipts, bank records showing rent paid, communications with your landlord about repairs or issues. If you do owe rent, knowing exactly how much and being able to show what you have paid is important.
5
Apply for emergency rental assistance immediately
Many cities and counties have emergency rental assistance funds. These programs can pay back rent and prevent eviction — but only if you apply in time. Call 211 and ask for "emergency rental assistance" in your area. Apply even if you are not sure you qualify.
6
Show up to court if there is a court date
If a court hearing is scheduled, you must appear — even if you do not have a lawyer yet, even if you are scared, even if you think you will lose. Not showing up almost always means automatic loss. If you have a lawyer or legal aid worker, they will tell you what to expect.
7
Document everything as it happens
Every conversation with your landlord. Every notice. Every text. Every voicemail. Date and time. This protects you and may matter in court.
What to SAY
1
Calling legal aid
"I just received an eviction notice. The deadline on the notice is [date]. I need legal help as soon as possible. Can you tell me whether I qualify for free legal services and what my next step should be?"
2
Calling 211 for rental assistance
"I received an eviction notice for unpaid rent. I need emergency rental assistance to keep my housing. Can you connect me with any programs in my area, and tell me what I need to apply?"
3
If your landlord contacts you
"I received the notice. I am working on this. I will be in touch through [text/email — pick written, not voice] going forward so we have a record of our conversations."
4
If a landlord tries to push you out illegally
"You cannot lock me out, remove my things, or shut off my utilities without a court order. If you do, I will report it. I am working with legal aid."
5
When the fear is overwhelming
"I have time. I am calling legal aid. I am not going to lose my home today just because I got this notice. One step at a time."
Critical resources — call today
Legal Services Corporation locator (free legal aid by zip code): lsc.gov/find-legal-aid
211 — emergency rental assistance + tenant resources in your area (call or text)
National Coalition for the Homeless tenant hotline information: nationalhomeless.org
If you have children in school, your district has a McKinney-Vento liaison who helps families facing housing instability — call your school
If you are a veteran, call the VA's National Call Center for Homeless Veterans: 1-877-424-3838
If you are pregnant or have a newborn, programs exist specifically to prevent eviction during this period — ask legal aid
A notice is the start. There is still time, and there is still help.
Getting an eviction notice is one of the most frightening pieces of mail a person can open. The fear is real — but the situation is not yet what you fear it will be. There is a legal process. There are protections. There is free help that knows this process inside and out and will fight for you. Most tenants who get legal help and apply for assistance keep their homes or get more time. The single most important thing you can do right now is call legal aid — and not leave your home before someone has helped you understand your rights. You are not alone in this. People help with this every day.

The Kind of Exhaustion That Feels Like Wanting to Quit Everything

This is not a normal tired. This is the kind of empty where the idea of getting up, doing the next thing, being the person everyone needs, feels not just hard but impossible. You might have felt this for a while. You might have hidden it from everyone. This page is not going to tell you five tricks to fix it — because that is not how this works. It is going to tell you what is real, what you are allowed to do, and where to find someone who can hold this with you.

Truth
What you are feeling is real. It is not weakness. It is not failure. It is depletion, and depletion is a signal, not a verdict.
Permission
You do not have to fix this today. You only have to not be alone with it.
If thoughts turn dark
If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or feel you cannot keep going, please call or text 988 right now. They will listen. You do not have to be in crisis to call.
What is real right now
1
Name it to yourself, gently
Not "I am broken" or "I am failing." Try: "I am depleted. I have been carrying something heavy for a long time and my body is telling me it needs help." Naming it accurately matters more than naming it bravely.
2
Tell one person — even if it's just to a hotline
This is not the moment to manage it alone. The point of telling someone is not to get a solution — it is to stop being alone inside the feeling. A trusted friend, a family member, a therapist, your doctor, or a hotline counselor. One person. Today if you can.
3
Reduce the load somewhere — even a little
Cancel something. Say no to one thing this week. Ask someone to handle one task you usually handle. You do not have to overhaul your life. You have to lower the water level so you can breathe.
4
Take care of your body in one small way today
Drink water. Eat something even if you are not hungry. Lie down without your phone for ten minutes. Step outside, even for two minutes. These are not solutions — they are signs to your body that you are paying attention.
5
If this has been going on for weeks or months, talk to a doctor or counselor
Long-term depletion can be a sign of burnout, depression, or a physical health issue — and there is real help for all of these. A doctor's appointment is not a commitment to anything. It is a conversation that opens doors. Tell them: "I have been exhausted in a way that does not lift. I want to figure out why."
6
If cost is the reason you have not gotten help
Call 211 and ask about free or sliding-scale mental health services in your area. Federally Qualified Health Centers (findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov) provide care regardless of ability to pay. Many therapists also offer reduced-fee sessions — you can ask.
What to say — to yourself, to someone else
1
To yourself when the voice gets loud
"I am not broken. I am depleted. There is a difference. Depletion is a signal that something needs to change, not that I am the thing that needs to change."
2
To someone you trust
"I need to tell you something I've been carrying. I am exhausted in a way I can't seem to push through. I'm not looking for advice right now — I just need someone to know."
3
Calling a doctor or therapist for the first time
"I would like to make an appointment. I have been struggling with persistent exhaustion and I'm not sure if it's physical, mental, or both — I want to figure it out."
4
If 988 feels like too much but you need to talk to someone
You can text 988 instead of calling. You can also call SAMHSA's helpline at 1-800-662-4357 for non-crisis mental health support and referrals. Both are free, confidential, and 24/7. You do not have to be in crisis to use them.
5
When someone asks how you are doing
You do not have to say "fine" if you are not. You can say: "Honestly, I've been having a rough stretch. I'm working on it." That is a complete answer. You do not owe more.
If you are in or near a crisis
Call or text 988 — Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Free, confidential, 24/7. You do not have to be in crisis to call. Counselors will listen without judgment
SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) — for mental health and substance use, including referrals to local treatment. Free, 24/7
If you are a veteran, the Veterans Crisis Line is 988, then press 1, or text 838255
If you are a caregiver, the Family Caregiver Alliance offers free support: caregiver.org
If you cannot afford care, Federally Qualified Health Centers (findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov) and Open Path Collective (openpathcollective.org) offer low-cost mental health services
You did not get here by being weak. You got here by carrying too much for too long.
The exhaustion you are feeling is not evidence of failure. It is evidence that you have been holding things together, for yourself and probably for other people, for longer than any human is supposed to do without help. The fact that you reached this page, that you read this far, means you are still trying. You do not have to push through. You do not have to be strong about this. You only have to take one small step — telling one person, making one call, lowering the load by one thing — and let that be enough for today. The rest can come tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that.
